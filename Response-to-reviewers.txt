Comments from the reviewers:
Reviewer #1 (Comments to the Author):
The manuscript covers a wide range of important topics, including organoid generation, niche
signaling, functional validation, tumor-derived organoids, Sjögren’s disease, radiation injury,
pharmacological platforms, regenerative transplantation, chemical reprogramming, extracellular
vesicles, and standardization. The review is ambitious and addresses an important unmet need in
the field.However, several conceptual and structural issues need to be addressed before the
manuscript can be considered suitable for publication in IJOS. In particular, the human versus
murine scope is not sufficiently clear, the three “unresolved tensions” introduced in the
Introduction are not consistently carried through the manuscript, and several conclusions rely on
evidence from non-salivary or non-human systems without adequate qualification.


Major comments
1. Clarify the human versus murine scope of the review
The title, abstract, and keywords frame the manuscript as a review on salivary gland organoids in
the context of precision medicine and regeneration, which implies a human-centered focus.
However, murine data are frequently interwoven with human data without explicit annotation or
discussion of translational relevance. Examples include:
Page 5–6: “Murine studies often use adult epithelium, while PSC-based approaches include
conditional blastocyst complementation…” This sentence appears abruptly and does not clearly
serve the argument at that point.
Page 11–13: The section “Species Differences: Murine versus Human Organoid Models” is
valuable, but it appears relatively late. By that point, readers have already encountered mixed
human and murine data in earlier sections.
Page 25–26: In the transplantation section, human salivary stem/progenitor cells, human
organoids transplanted into NOD mice, murine SGO allogeneic transplantation, and the Dutch
RESTART clinical program are discussed together. It is not always clear which findings are
human-relevant preclinical evidence and which are mouse proof-of-concept.
Page 31: Scaffold-guided spheroid engineering and EV studies are largely murine or in vitro;
placing them under “Future Perspectives and Emerging Technologies” may lead readers to
overestimate their clinical readiness.
Response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting the need to clarify the species-specific scope
and translational relevance of the evidence discussed throughout the review. We have
strengthened the distinction between murine and human experimental systems while retaining

available evidence spans murine developmental and preclinical models, human organoid cultures
and human-derived grafts evaluated in mouse recipients. This provides an early, up-front

dedicated comparison and flagged at the point of first use throughout the intervening sections.
In the “Cell Sources” subsection, we explicitly distinguish human iPSC-based approaches from

3. Strengthen the critical discussion of human acinar maturation

a.The attribution of ductal dominance primarily to WNT/EGF-rich expansion media is plausible
but incomplete.
b.Murine acinar competence should not be presented as a uniform standard. Murine organoids
also vary by gland of origin, protocol, passage number, and Matrigel batch. Please avoid
implying that mouse is the “gold standard” and human is the “defective” system.
c.The statement that human organoids are “not intrinsically less competent” is reasonable but
currently more inferential than demonstrated. Please either provide direct supporting evidence or
frame it more cautiously.
d.For DAPT/Notch inhibition, please discuss the stability of the effect, potential effects on other
lineages, and off-target concerns.

ONCOLOGY,” the authors describe organoids derived from various histological types of
salivary gland tumors and summarize the relevant studies in Table 3. However, the subsequent
discussion of their applications remains relatively general. Given the substantial histological
and molecular heterogeneity of salivary gland tumors, it would be valuable to further
discuss whether organoids derived from different tumor subtypes have distinct applications,
particularly in disease modeling, drug screening, and personalized therapeutic evaluation.

Response: We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We have expanded the “Precision
Medicine: Drug Screening and Therapeutic Stratification” subsection to clarify how the
applications and current evidence for PDOs differ across salivary gland tumor subtypes.

In addition to the established example of TRK inhibitor testing in ETV6–NTRK3-positive
secretory carcinoma, we now discuss genotype-informed drug screening in salivary duct
carcinoma, including the limitation of incomplete HER2 retention in some organoid cultures. We
further distinguish proposed AREG–EGFR-directed screening in mucoepidermoid carcinoma
and molecularly informed disease modeling in adenoid cystic carcinoma from applications
already demonstrated in salivary gland PDOs.
We also highlight the challenges of slow-growing and rare tumor subtypes, for which
representative model establishment and molecular authentication remain important prerequisites.
These additions clarify the distinct opportunities and limitations of subtype-specific PDO
applications without implying that their clinical predictive utility has been established. These
considerations complement the subtype-specific tumor characteristics summarized in Table 3 and
the PDO authentication criteria provided in the newly added Table 4.

2.Salivary gland function depends not only on acinar epithelial cells but also on coordinated
interactions among epithelial, neural, stromal, and immune cells, as well as biochemical and
mechanical signals from the extracellular matrix. The authors are encouraged to further discuss
relevant studies investigating these cellular and microenvironmental interactions during in
vitro organoid culture and following in vivo transplantation, particularly their potential roles in
organoid maturation, functional integration, and secretory activity.
Response: We thank the reviewer for emphasizing the importance of cellular and
microenvironmental interactions in salivary gland organoid maturation and function. We have
expanded the discussion to distinguish experimental evidence obtained during in vitro culture
from observations following transplantation.
First, we have incorporated primary studies demonstrating that PDGFRα-positive stromal cells
promote proacinar differentiation through FGF2-associated BMP signaling in murine embryonic
salivary organoids, whereas endothelial cells and VEGFR2 signaling influence epithelial
patterning in developing mouse salivary gland models. We have also included our recently
accepted study of murine chimeric salivary organoids, which incorporates multiple stromal
populations and demonstrates changes in inflammatory signaling and selected acinar-associated
markers following pharmacological perturbation (Yoon et al., J Dent Res, in press). This model
provides direct salivary gland evidence of multicellular stromal–epithelial interactions, although
its functional relevance to human disease remains unverified. We have additionally expanded the
discussion of neural interactions by distinguishing engineered human dental-pulp-derived
salivary-like constructs and murine salivary organoid–neuronal co-cultures from models with
established long-term functional innervation.


Second, we have clarified how ECM degradability, composition and mechanical properties
influence epithelial polarization and acinar-associated phenotypes, drawing on experimental
hydrogel studies in murine and human salivary cell systems.
Third, in the transplantation section, we now specify that co-transplantation with embryonic
murine salivary mesenchyme enhanced acinar-like differentiation of human organoid grafts in an
ectopic mouse model. We distinguish these findings, including observed vascularization, from
evidence of mature neural or immune integration and functional drainage into the oral cavity.
Finally, as discussed in the Sjögren's disease section, immune–epithelial integration remains a
prospective direction for human salivary gland organoids, informed by native gland profiling and
immune-integrated organoid studies in other tissues.
Together, these revisions provide a more evidence-based account of how stromal, neural,
vascular and extracellular-matrix cues contribute to organoid maturation and function, while
clarifying the limitations of currently available models.

“Organoid Transplantation: From Preclinical Validation to Clinical Trials”; and Pages 34–35,
“Multicellular System Integration.”



3.In addition to achieving successful engraftment and functional integration, transplanted
salivary gland organoids may face challenges related to immune compatibility, host immune
responses, and potential immune-mediated tissue damage. These considerations may be
particularly important in autoimmune conditions such as Sjögren's disease, where the underlying
pathological immune microenvironment may persist after transplantation. The authors are
encouraged to expand the discussion of these challenges and potential strategies to improve
graft survival, functional integration, and long-term transplantation outcomes.
Response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting immune compatibility as an important
determinant of long-term graft survival and functional integration.
We have expanded the “Organoid Transplantation: From Preclinical Validation to Clinical
Trials” subsection to distinguish alloimmune rejection from recurrent autoimmune injury.
Although autologous products reduce donor–recipient alloimmune mismatch, they may remain
susceptible to the inflammatory microenvironment associated with Sjögren's disease.

We now discuss the available transplantation evidence in this context. Human organoids
improved salivary secretion in autoimmune-prone NOD mice seven weeks after transplantation,
despite unchanged lymphocytic focus scores. However, this short-term improvement does not
demonstrate durable protection against immune-mediated graft injury. We also clarify that
findings from immunodeficient xenotransplantation models and murine transplantation studies
cannot establish long-term immune compatibility in human recipients.

Finally, we discuss potential approaches, including donor–recipient compatibility assessment,
local or disease-directed immunomodulation, and longitudinal monitoring of graft persistence,
immune infiltration, epithelial injury and salivary function. We emphasize that these strategies
remain to be validated specifically for salivary organoid transplantation.
